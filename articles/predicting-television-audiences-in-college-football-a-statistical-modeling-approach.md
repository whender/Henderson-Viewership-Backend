title: "PREDICTING TELEVISION AUDIENCES IN COLLEGE FOOTBALL: A STATISTICAL MODELING APPROACH"

description: "This paper explores how statistical modeling can predict college football television audiences before kickoff using game, team, and scheduling characteristics."

---
College football has become one of the most economically impactful television forces in the United States, where conference media deals have approached and surpassed $1 billion in value in recent years. Networks, conferences, universities, advertisers, and local communities all make decisions based on expectations about audience size. Those expectations influence kickoff times, network assignments, media-rights negotiations, conference realignment strategy, and the visibility of individual institutions. This paper asks a practical research question: how accurately can college football television viewership be predicted from information available before a game is played?

To answer that question, I assembled an original dataset containing 2,389 nationally televised FBS games and developed a regression-based forecasting model using publicly available television audience and game data. The dependent variable is total Persons 2+ viewership, modeled on a logarithmic scale so that the model estimates proportional changes in audience size rather than treating a 200,000-viewer miss on a small cable game the same as a 200,000-viewer miss on a major broadcast window. Predictions are converted back into viewers with a smearing adjustment to reduce retransformation bias.

The model combines several categories of predictors. The first is distribution: whether a game aired on ABC, FOX, CBS, NBC, ESPN2, FS1, BTN, CW, ESPNU, FS2, NFL Network, or ESPNNEWS. The second is scheduling context, including day of week, Saturday window, Friday games, Black Friday, Week 0, Week 1, and whether the game was played outside a standard Saturday slot. The third is competitive salience: AP-style ranking categories, conference championship games, whether both teams are ranked, conference-specific postseason implications, and pregame team records to date. The fourth is rivalry and brand strength. The model includes fixed effects for teams with enough observations, plus specific rivalry indicators such as Michigan-Ohio State, Texas-Oklahoma, Alabama-Auburn, BYU-Utah, and other recurring games whose audiences may exceed what rankings and network alone would imply. Finally, the model estimates a "competing games score," which measures the predicted strength of other games airing within approximately 90 minutes of the same kickoff. This recognizes that a broadcast's audience depends not only on its own attractiveness, but also on the alternative games available at the same time.

This structure reflects a central feature of sports media markets: viewership is jointly produced by team brands, competitive stakes, distribution, and scarcity. A ranked game on a major broadcast network is valuable partly because it is easy to find and partly because viewers believe it matters. But those same viewers may split attention if another high-brand or high-stakes game airs simultaneously. The model therefore treats college football as a schedule-level prediction problem, not simply a matchup-level prediction problem.

The model performs well enough to be useful, while still showing the uncertainty inherent in live sports. In ten random 80/20 holdout tests, the pregame model produced an average mean absolute error of about 471,000 viewers and a log-scale R-squared of .922. The median absolute percentage error was 21.9 percent, with 54.9 percent of games predicted within 25 percent of the actual audience and 83.5 percent within 50 percent. A more difficult leave-one-season-out test, which trains on all seasons except one and predicts the excluded year, produced an average mean absolute error of 489,000 viewers and a log-scale R-squared of .904. A forward-chain test, which uses only earlier seasons to predict later seasons, produced an average mean absolute error of 524,000 viewers and a log-scale R-squared of .893.

Those results suggest two conclusions. First, a large share of college football viewership is explainable before kickoff. Network, time slot, rankings, team brands, conference context, and schedule competition carry substantial predictive information. Second, the model's errors are not failures so much as evidence of the limits of prediction. Injuries, weather, in-game competitiveness, upset potential, playoff committee narratives, and social media attention can all shift audiences in ways that are difficult to know before kickoff. A postgame version of the model, which adds final score differential, improves performance modestly: its random holdout mean absolute error falls to about 444,000 viewers and its leave-one-season-out error to about 459,000. The smaller improvement implies that much of the audience is already determined by pregame expectations and distribution, although game quality still matters.

The model also produces interpretable brand-power estimates. In the current version, teams such as Ohio State, Alabama, Michigan, Georgia, Florida, Nebraska, Penn State, Notre Dame, LSU, Colorado, Texas, Oklahoma, Tennessee, Florida State, and Auburn rank near the top after controlling for other factors. These estimates should not be read as pure fan-base size. They are better understood as television draw: the extra audience associated with a team after accounting for network, time slot, rankings, rivalry, conference context, and other observable factors. This distinction matters because media value is not identical to athletic success. A program can be competitively strong without being an unusually strong national television draw, and a program with a large brand can continue to attract audiences even in less successful seasons.

For universities, the implications extend beyond athletics departments. Television exposure affects applications, alumni engagement, donor attention, and the public image of a campus. For conferences, viewership models can help evaluate which matchups deserve premium windows and how realignment changes media value.

For policymakers and public institutions, the model highlights how public universities participate in a media economy where decisions about scheduling and conference affiliation can affect regional economies, student recruitment, and institutional resources. Sports media rights may appear separate from public policy, but they intersect with antitrust questions, labor issues, public universities, state identity, and the economic geography of higher education.

The model has limitations. Its data are based on nationally rated games, so it is strongest for televised games with reported audience figures and weaker for games outside that universe. Because the media environment has changed rapidly, especially with streaming, conference realignment, and Nielsen methodology changes, past relationships may not perfectly describe future seasons. The model also uses engineered variables that require judgment, such as which rivalries deserve separate indicators and how to define competing games. Those choices improve interpretability but should be tested continuously.

Even with those limitations, the project shows that college football viewership can be studied rigorously with statistical tools. The most valuable result is not a single prediction, but a framework for thinking about why audiences form. Viewership is not random. It reflects brand loyalty, competitive stakes, distribution power, scarcity, and the structure of the schedule itself. A statistical model cannot capture every cultural and emotional reason people watch college football, but it can clarify which factors consistently move audiences and how much uncertainty remains. That makes it useful not only for sports analytics, but also for understanding how data, media institutions, and public universities interact in modern American life.
